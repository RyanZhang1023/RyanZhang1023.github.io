Custom Guitar Overdrive Pedal

I built a custom guitar overdrive pedal, displayed in the figure below, based on Klon Centaur’s circuit. This project involved PCB design using Kicad, electronic assembly and soldering using utilities provided by TechSpark, and enclosure design using FreeCAD. I fabricated the enclosure using 3D printing and incorporated heat-set threaded inserts to create reusable screw connections.

I chose to reproduce Klon Centaur because of its renowned craftsmanship that enticed numerous professional guitarists, such as Jeff Beck and John Mayer, to incorporate it in their pedalboards. The pedal, developed by Bill Finnegan in the 90s, pioneered “transparent” ovedrive by boosting gain without affecting a guitar’s natural tone (Warren).

PCB Design

My pedal replicates Klon Centaur overdrive pedal, and its breakdown analysis is available online (“Klon Centaur Analysis · ElectroSmash Archive”). I translated the reference circuit into a PCB layout, selecting component footprints and arranging them for physical assembly. The reproduced schematic is shown in Figure 2.

Notably, this schematic does not have a true bypass – that is, even when the pedal is turned off, the signal still passes through the input buffer and two high-pass filters.

Then, I assigned footprints for specific electronic components. For example, I used NMJ6HCD2 for the input and output jacks and implemented PJ-102AH for the power jack. I also selected a 3PDT footswitch, but I did not initially find a suitable footprint that matched the physical switch I purchased. This mismatch later created an assembly challenge.

I updated the PCB from schematic and made all the electronic component fit in a rectangular board, as shown in Figure 3.

However, I quickly came to realize that in this design, multiple components were placed too close to each other, which might pose difficulties when it comes to wiring and soldering. Therefore, I designed another PCB, as shown in Figure 4 below, such that adequate spacing of electronics was ensured.

All the components fit in a 90mm x 100mm rectangular board. I routed most of the connections on the front copper layer and used vias where necessary to route connections around other traces. I also included two M3 mounting holes in opposite corners to secure the PCB inside the enclosure.

Electronic Assembly

After finishing the PCB design, I submitted it to be manufactured and assembled by JLCPCB. While most components could be assembled, some could not.

As shown in Figure 5, three potentiometers, a switch, a MAX1044 chip, and two diodes were left unassembled. I bought the remaining components from Tayda Electronics and used equipment from TechSpark to complete the remaining through-hole and hardware integration.

During assembly, I discovered that the PCB footprint for the footswitch did not match the physical footswitch I had purchased. To work around this mismatch, I used nine female jumper wires to connect the switch terminals to the corresponding PCB connections, as shown in Figure 7 below.

I tested the pedal out with my own guitar, but it did not produce the expected distortion. I looked back to check the schematics and found out that two nets that were supposed to be linked were separated.

Although +4.5V and +4V5 represent the same nominal voltage, KiCad treated them as different net labels, as shown in Figure 8. Consequently, the corresponding parts of the circuit were not electrically connected. To correct this issue, I soldered a wire to the back of the PCB to bridge the two nets, as shown in Figure 9.

I connected one end of the wire to a pad on the +4.5V net and the other end to a pad on the +4V5 net. After making this connection, I tested the pedal again and confirmed that it worked as expected.

Enclosure Design

I designed a box and a front lid for the guitar pedal to protect the electronics and make the pedal easier to transport.

In Figure 10, I measured the dimensions of the PCB to determine the internal layout of the box. The two sides of the box had a U-shape cutout to leave room for the input and output jacks. I incorporated a separate opening for the power jack. I designed two mounting posts on the enclosure floor to support and secure the PCB. Finally, I incorporated screw holes at the four corners to accommodate heat-set threaded inserts, allowing the lid to be fastened securely to the enclosure.

As shown in Figure 11, I designed openings in the lid for the footswitch and potentiometers so that the controls could be accessed from outside the enclosure. However, the potentiometers I purchased were too short to protrude through the lid, leaving them recessed inside the enclosure.

After completing the enclosure and lid designs in FreeCAD, I fabricated both parts using a 3D printer.

Using a soldering iron, I installed six heat-set threaded inserts into the PLA enclosure. These inserts provide reusable threaded connections, allowing the enclosure to be opened and reassembled without relying on screws threading directly into the plastic.

Finally, I placed the PCB inside the enclosure and secured it with screws, completing the assembly of the pedal.

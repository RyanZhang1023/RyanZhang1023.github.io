Custom Guitar Overdrive Pedal

Tags: PCB Design, Electronics Assembly, CAD, 3D Printing

I built a custom guitar overdrive pedal based on the Klon Centaur circuit. This project involved PCB design in KiCad, electronic assembly and soldering using equipment at TechSpark, and enclosure design in FreeCAD. I fabricated the enclosure using 3D printing and incorporated heat-set threaded inserts to create reusable screw connections.

I chose to reproduce the Klon Centaur because of its renowned craftsmanship and popularity among professional guitarists, including Jeff Beck and John Mayer. Developed by Bill Finnegan in the 1990s, the Klon Centaur became known for its “transparent” overdrive, which boosts a guitar’s signal while aiming to preserve its natural tonal character (Warren).

PCB Design

My pedal reproduces the Klon Centaur circuit, whose operation is analyzed in the online resource Klon Centaur Analysis (“Klon Centaur Analysis · ElectroSmash Archive”). I translated the reference circuit into a PCB layout, selecting component footprints and arranging components for physical assembly.

Notably, the circuit does not provide true bypass. Even when the pedal is switched off, the signal still passes through the input buffer and two high-pass filters.

I assigned footprints to the electronic components, including NMJ6HCD2 jacks for audio input and output and a PJ-102AH connector for the power supply. I also selected a 3PDT footswitch, but I did not initially find a suitable footprint that matched the physical switch I purchased. This mismatch later created an assembly challenge.

I converted the schematic into a PCB layout and arranged the components on a rectangular board.

After reviewing the initial layout, I realized that several components were positioned too close together, which could make wiring and soldering difficult. I therefore revised the layout to provide more space between components and improve accessibility during assembly.

The final PCB measured 90 mm × 100 mm. I routed most connections on the front copper layer and used vias where necessary to route connections around other traces. I also included two M3 mounting holes in opposite corners to secure the PCB inside the enclosure.

Electronic Assembly

After completing the PCB design, I submitted the board to JLCPCB for fabrication and assembly. However, several components could not be assembled as part of the service.

Three potentiometers, the footswitch, the MAX1044 IC, and two diodes were left unassembled. I purchased the remaining components from Tayda Electronics and used equipment at TechSpark to complete the remaining through-hole soldering and hardware integration.

During assembly, I discovered that the PCB footprint for the footswitch did not match the physical switch I had purchased. To work around this mismatch, I used nine female jumper wires to connect the switch terminals to the corresponding PCB connections.

I tested the pedal with my guitar, but it did not produce the expected overdrive effect. After reviewing the schematic and PCB connections, I discovered that two nets that should have been connected were treated as separate nets.

Although +4.5V and +4V5 represent the same nominal voltage, KiCad treated them as different net labels. Consequently, the corresponding parts of the circuit were not electrically connected. To correct this issue, I soldered a wire to the back of the PCB to bridge the two nets.

I connected one end of the wire to a pad on the +4.5V net and the other end to a pad on the +4V5 net. After making this connection, I tested the pedal again and confirmed that it worked as expected.

Enclosure Design

I designed a custom enclosure consisting of a box and a lid to protect the electronics and make the pedal easier to transport.

I measured the PCB and used its dimensions to determine the enclosure’s internal layout. I incorporated U-shaped cutouts into two sides of the enclosure to accommodate the audio input and output jacks, along with a separate opening for the power jack. I designed two mounting posts on the enclosure floor to support and secure the PCB. I also incorporated screw holes at the four corners for heat-set threaded inserts, allowing the lid to be fastened securely.

I designed openings in the lid for the footswitch and potentiometers so that the controls could be accessed from outside the enclosure. However, the potentiometers I purchased were too short to protrude through the lid, leaving them recessed inside the enclosure.

After completing the enclosure and lid designs in FreeCAD, I fabricated both parts using a 3D printer.

Using a soldering iron, I installed six heat-set threaded inserts into the PLA enclosure. These inserts provide reusable threaded connections, allowing the enclosure to be opened and reassembled without relying on screws threaded directly into the plastic.

Finally, I placed the PCB inside the enclosure and secured it with screws, completing the assembly of the pedal.
